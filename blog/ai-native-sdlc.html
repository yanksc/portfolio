The short answer

The core ideaGive every engineer a sanctioned coding agent, then spend most of your effort on context, review and release. AI now raises delivery throughput, but it still lowers delivery stability, and the bottleneck moves from writing code to reviewing and shipping it.

  • Start here, days 0–30. Publish an AI stance covering allowed tools, data rules and expectations. Give every builder an agentic coding tool. Route all MCP traffic through one gateway with no personal access tokens. Measure lead time by stage, change-fail rate and AI cost before you change anything.
  • Build only the last mile. Build a team plugin (agent config per repo, skills, runbooks) and your own eval sets. Buy commodity agent tooling, and adopt what your platform already runs.
  • Run seven workstreams. Guardrails, context, intent to spec, spec to PR, agentic quality, release to recovery, and people with measurement. Each has an outcome and key results.
  • Rank tools with one formula. Reach × hours saved × bottleneck weight × confidence, divided by effort × risk. Connect source control, issue tracker, docs, metrics, chat and design first; start agents on code review, build failures, on-call triage and ticket-to-PR.
  • Ramp on evidence. Four phases over 12 months, each closed by a measured gate, with change-fail rate guarding every speed target. Assign a shared enablement owner and local team champions.

What AI-native means

AI-native means an agent is the default starting point for every SDLC step (spec, code, test, review, release, operate), while people set intent, review the output and own the outcome.

Tools alone don’t get you there. DORA’s 2025 research, drawn from nearly 5,000 respondents, describes AI as an amplifier of whatever system it lands in. It names seven capabilities that decide whether AI helps: a clear AI stance, healthy data, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control, small batches, user-centric focus and quality internal platforms. Without a user-centric focus, AI adoption can lower team performance (DORA AI Capabilities Model).

Place each team on this ladder. The exit gates are proposed targets; set your own once you have a baseline.

An org that has handed out licenses but not yet built context, review capacity or measurement sits between L1 and L2.

The capability map

An AI-native SDLC needs agents at five stages and six shared capabilities beneath them. When each team wires up agents on its own, every team re-solves auth, tool access, sandboxing and audit, and none of it gets reviewed.

Only two layers are yours to build, because only you hold your product’s context and know what a correct result looks like in your domain. DORA found that a quality internal platform amplifies AI’s effect on organizational performance, so put the shared capabilities on the platform instead of in each team.

Workstreams, outcomes and key results

Seven workstreams cover the loop. Targets are proposals for a two to three quarter horizon; measure baselines in the first 30 days before you commit to them.

  1. A

    Guardrails and AI stance

    OutcomeEveryone knows what is allowed, and agents act with scoped, audited access.

    • AI stance published by week 2
    • 100% of agent tool calls through the MCP gateway; zero personal access tokens in agent configs
    • AI cost per merged PR reported monthly
  2. B

    Context and agent readiness

    OutcomeAgents get your org’s context without a person relaying it.

    • 100% of active repos carry an agent config file and the team plugin
    • Top 5 agent use cases finish end to end without human rescue in 4 of 5 trials
    • Average human intervention per agent task halved
  3. C

    Intent to spec

    OutcomeIdeas become agent-ready specs fast, and PMs and designers ship small UI changes themselves.

    • Idea-to-approved-spec time halved
    • 90%+ of specs pass review the first time
    • Spec rework under 10%
  4. D

    Spec to PR

    OutcomeAgents write most of the code, and review stops being the bottleneck.

    • Median PR review time halved from baseline
    • Median PR size flat or smaller
    • One new auto-merge PR class live each quarter
  5. E

    Agentic quality and CI

    OutcomeMore changes ship without more failures.

    • Build-failure remediation agent on every repo
    • Flaky-test rate halved
    • Change-fail rate at or below baseline while deploy frequency doubles
  6. F

    Release to recovery

    OutcomeShipping and operating are as agent-assisted as coding.

    • Deploy stage of lead time cut by half
    • Risk-scored progressive delivery on every tier-1 service
    • On-call triage agent drafts the first response on 80% of pages; MTTR down 30%
  7. G

    People, proficiency and measurement

    OutcomeAI-native is how the org works, and you can prove it.

    • 100% of engineers at L2 or above, 50% at L3, 20% at L4
    • Weekly AI usage at 90%+
    • Lead time by stage reviewed monthly against AI cost

Staff it lean. A shared AI enablement owner coordinates A, B and G and runs the weekly cadence. Local champions drive adoption within their teams. Size the effort to your organization and its constraints. Teams own C through F, and the pod removes their blockers.

MCP servers and agents: what to connect first

Keep in mindConnect read-only context servers first, route write access only through pipelines, and build your own MCP server last. Score every candidate server, skill or agent with one formula, so the order is argued from data rather than enthusiasm.

Priority = (Reach × Hours saved per use × Bottleneck weight × Confidence)
           ÷ (Effort × Risk)
Reach
Uses or runs per week across the org.
Hours saved
Hours saved per use, measured on a sample of real tasks.
Bottleneck weight
Share of lead time at the stage the tool acts on, from 0 to 1.
Confidence
1.0 for a proven product or existing platform feature; about 0.5 for something you build.
Effort
Person-weeks to roll it out to the whole org.
Risk
1 for read-only, 2 for writes behind review, 3 for direct production writes.

Treat tool descriptions and tool output as untrusted input. The OWASP MCP Top 10 lists token mismanagement, scope creep, tool poisoning and shadow servers among the main risks. Pin server versions, allowlist servers from a registry, and run untrusted code in sandboxes (OWASP MCP Top 10 guide).

Which agents first

Start with agents that run often on bounded work and are easy to verify: AI code review on every PR, build-failure remediation, first-draft on-call triage, and ticket-to-PR for well-specified tickets. Leave open-ended feature agents until your evals and review capacity can keep up with them.

Prioritization and roadmap

Prioritize by where the time goes. Before you buy anything, measure lead time by stage: prep, code, review and deploy. Telemetry across thousands of teams shows that as code generation speeds up, the constraint shifts to review, integration and deployment (Faros).

Apply five rules, in order:

  1. Foundations gate everything. No agent rollout before the AI stance, gateway-only tool access and baselines exist.
  2. Attack the biggest stage first. In most orgs that is review or deploy, not coding.
  3. Buy or adopt before you build. A proven product scores 1.0 on confidence; a custom build scores about 0.5.
  4. Read before write, bounded before open-ended. Agents start on read-only context and well-defined work such as build fixes, test gaps and migrations.
  5. Ramp on evidence. Every phase ends at a gate, and a missed gate means slow down or hold.

Everything in the top-left quadrant can be bought or configured, so the first 90 days cost habit change rather than engineering.

Confirm the gate targets against your baselines at Gate 1. The weekly check keeps the ramp honest: all targets met means continue, one miss means slow down, and a blocker or two misses means hold.

Continue

Targets met. Quality holds.

Slow down

One miss. Investigate before expanding.

Hold

A blocker or repeated misses. Fix first.

Measurement

Keep in mindMeasure flow end to end and pair every speed metric with a quality guardrail. Adoption is an input, never the goal.

Self-reports mislead. In METR’s early-2025 randomized trial, experienced developers felt 20% faster with AI but were 19% slower (METR 2025). METR’s 2026 follow-up says newer agentic tools likely do speed developers up, and still calls self-reported gains unreliable (METR 2026).

Report at the level of the teams that ship together, rather than by repo or reporting line. Faros found that aggregate numbers hide which teams AI is helping and which it is hurting.

Risks and guardrails

The failure to plan for is not low adoption. It is faster code feeding a review queue and a release process that cannot absorb it.
